**Reading.** With obstacles in a realistic mixed order, the device starts at the generic level (72% over the first 5 crossings), improves steadily while it learns the person (79% for crossings 6-15) and reaches 90% from crossing 16 on. In the blocked study order the early history covers only one or two obstacles, so the person scaling is biased and accuracy collapses for crossings 6-15 (51%) before catching up (84% from crossing 16 on, once 3-4 obstacles have been seen). The blocked-order "learning during use" numbers elsewhere in this project are therefore pessimistic for real use, where obstacles come mixed.

## R2. A virtual real-time cueing device

**(a) Detection at the lead foot apex.** Over all 7250 strides of the sessions (online features available at the apex: peak height, time and distance to the apex, vertical speed, height 0.1 s before; scaled to the person's walking): 99.0% of the leading-leg crossing strides are detected at their apex; 30 ordinary strides are falsely detected (3.0 per session of ~725 strides).

**(b, c) Height and cue.** Leading-leg data up to the cue moment (approach strides + the lead swing curves cut at the cue), generic model for the first 5 crossings, then person scaling from the earlier crossings (mixed order: mean of 30 random orders; blocked: real order). **Reading.** In real time and without a camera, the lead-foot sensor finds the crossing at its apex (99%), and 450 ms before the trailing toe leaves the floor the device gives a correct height cue for 71% of the crossings over a whole mixed session (76% once it has seen 15 crossings). Only leading-leg data exist at that moment, so the real-time cue is less accurate than the after-the-fact recognition; a confidence threshold of 0.6 raises the share of right cues from 72% to 79%, at the cost of withholding 30% of the cues. Waiting for the lead landing leaves only 200 ms. False detections on ordinary strides (~3 per session) would need a second check in a product (e.g. confirm when the trailing foot starts its swing).

Correlation over the 10 participants: person-scaled accuracy vs lead d' r = 0.25, vs trail d' r = 0.77; generic accuracy vs lead d' r = 0.01, vs trail d' r = -0.66. **Reading.** Once the device is scaled to the person, its errors follow how consistently that person lifts the TRAILING foot for a given height: the weakest participant (108, 73%) has the least consistent trailing lift (d' 0.9).
