## 3. Crossing strategy

### 3.1 Dose-response: change per cm of obstacle height (nominal height from the measured leg length)

| trail shank backward tilt (deg) | -1.1 | -1.7 to -0.2 | 0 / 10 | 0.73 |

**Reading.** Foot lift tracks the obstacle almost one-to-one: +10.5 mm (lead) and +11.3 mm (trail) per cm of obstacle (R^2 0.87 / 0.85, positive in every participant), driven by +1.2 deg of lead thigh flexion and -1.1 deg of trailing shank backward tilt per cm. The camera clearance margin, in contrast, hardly depends on the height (-1.4 and -1.1 mm per cm, R^2 0.12 / 0.05): people raise the foot by about the obstacle height and keep a roughly constant safety margin, slightly smaller for taller obstacles. The wearables therefore see the obstacle mainly through how much the foot is lifted, not through the margin.

### 3.4 The personal obstacle margin - why a generic model tops out

Per participant, the foot lift predicted by their own dose-response line at the cohort's mean obstacle height ("personal margin"). Its spread across people is compared with the step between neighbouring heights (median slope x 10 % of the leg length), and correlated with what a device could know without crossings (the person's level-walk value of the same variable, leg length).

| variable | between-person SD | step between heights | SD / step | r with level-walk value | r with leg length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| lead foot lift | 64 mm | 98 mm | 0.66 | 0.43 | 0.20 |
| trail foot lift | 76 mm | 105 mm | 0.72 | -0.22 | 0.04 |

**Reading.** People differ in how much extra they lift for ANY obstacle by about two-thirds of the step between two heights, and this personal margin is hardly predictable from ordinary walking or body size. A model that has never seen the person therefore confuses neighbouring heights; a few of the person's own crossings (labelled or not) remove the problem - the reason for the ~62-73% generic vs ~90% personalised accuracy.

**Reading.** Beyond the shared obstacle, the two legs vary independently (|r| <~ 0.1): the leading leg tells the device how high the obstacle is, but not whether the trailing foot will under-lift on this crossing.
