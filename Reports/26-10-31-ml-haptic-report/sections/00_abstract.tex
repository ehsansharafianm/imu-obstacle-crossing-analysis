Ten young adults crossed obstacles of three heights (10, 20 and 30\,\% of leg length) and two widths (5 and 15\,cm) while wearing
six Movella Dot IMUs (thigh, shank and foot of both legs), with camera motion capture as reference. From the processed recordings
a crossing-level dataset was built (349 rows: 303 obstacle crossings, 46 level walks; 260 crossings used for machine learning), and
twelve MATLAB analyses were run, every accuracy being measured on a participant left out of training. People lift each foot by about
the obstacle height (10.1\,mm per cm for the leading foot, 95\,\% CI 9.4--10.8) and keep their safety margin (the leading-foot clearance stays within 2.6\,cm while the obstacle rises by 18.5\,cm; the trailing
one tends to fall slightly); of the 26 parameters of the step~9 group analysis, height changes six per limb after a false-discovery-rate
correction, most clearly the leading-thigh and trailing-shank tilts. Each person
has a strong walking signature and a personal obstacle margin. Generic models therefore recognise the obstacle height of a new
person in only 56--73\,\% of crossings (chance 33\,\%), whereas scaling the features with the person's own crossings, without any
label, gives 90--92\,\%; two foot IMUs are enough (92.3\,\%, 95\,\% CI 87--96; six IMUs are not significantly better), the
height can be estimated in centimetres with a 2.4\,cm error, and a camera-free pipeline that finds the crossings, pairs the two
limbs and classifies the height is fully correct for 86\,\% of the crossings. The result needs no laboratory hardware: sampling at
10\,Hz costs at most about 3 points, the noise of a consumer-grade IMU nothing, and a 10-tree forest of about 3\,kB keeps 91\,\%.
Obstacle width is not recognisable (54--66\,\%, chance 50\,\%): a deeper obstacle raises the foot by only 25\,mm, consistently
across people but little against the 40\,mm crossing-to-crossing variation. \input{sections/00b_abstract_haptic}
